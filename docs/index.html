Hero/Myth

For centuries, sailors have told legends about whales battling giant monsters beneath the waves. Breaching and beached sperm whales bore the scars of battles in the deep dark, sparking myths of lurking behemoths that lingered long past the age of dinosaurs. Predating modern marine science, Aristotle described a giant squid, and the Norse told the legend of the Kraken. But sailors could only describe what they could see - strange beaks, scars on whales and giant tentacles rising out of the water.

Today, we know that there are real animals behind these myths: Meet the sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus) and giant squid (Archetuethis dux). The real story is much more interesting: Both animals are locked in an evolutionary arms race, constantly adapting to better capture prey and evade other predators. Our subjects are historically misunderstood animals that are nearly opposites of each other, showcasing a spectrum of survival tools millions of years in the making.

How to Follow a Whale

01

Meet the Scientists

Let’s take a look at what modern-day cetologists want to learn more about sperm whale hunting behavior. Wait, what’s a “cetologist?” Over the past several hundred years, the field of marine biology has branched into many topics, including very specialized topics, like the study of whales or squid. A cetologist is simply a person who studies whales. A teuthologist is a person who studies things like squid and octopuses. Studying creatures that are foreign to us can help us answer the deepest questions about what really goes in some of the most mysterious parts of our planet.

02

The Challenge

But the sea does not give up secrets easily. Sperm whales hunt for squid largely in the mesopelagic zone, which is 200 - 1000 meters deep. Extreme temperatures and high pressure make it extremely challenging to understand how sperm whales and giant squid interact. Cetologist teams have developed some specialized tools for these expeditions. Some tools are very advanced, like an underwater camera that could withstand high pressures with several different types of sensors on it. Other tools are surprisingly simple - like a stick with a suction cup on the end that would firmly attach the deep-sea camera onto a sperm whale.

03

Adapting the Tools

Consider animals that you see every day- it could be birds in your backyard, pond wildlife at your local park, or family pets. Every day, we observe them solving problems, playing, and hunting. Tools such as binoculars, camera collars, magnifying glasses, microphones, drones, and motion-sensing trail cameras can uncover secrets of their hidden lives. How do you think we can apply these same types of tools to studying marine wildlife?

Similar equipment can help us see and hear sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus ) underwater, but this comes with many challenges than studying animals on land.

For starters, cetologists need to be able to attach this equipment to a living, wild whale! Once their equipment is attached, it needs to be able to survive ruthless ocean conditions like pressure, extreme temperatures, and high salinity.

The Science of Survival

Cetologists use advanced technology to understand not just who these animals are, but how they use communication, perception, and specialized tools to survive. One species is born with skills to evade capture, and one that passes those lessons down through generations. It turns out that these legendary “sea monsters” of old are both strong survivors in a changing world. Let’s look at the evolutionary arms race between these seemingly-opposite masters of the deep:

Squid have cantaloupe-sized eyes to see further in the darkness than their competitors. Scanning the darkness with the largest eyes in the animal kingdom, giant squids (Architeuthis dux) focus on bioluminescent trails.

They’re able to see the “soup” of bioluminescent plankton that is disturbed and illuminated by a pod of diving sperm whales, like the spinning, rolling wake behind a speedboat or a flurry of snowflakes.

A large disturbance suggests a large animal approaching, so the squid must decide to freeze in place or flee with jet propulsion, leaving a cloud of ink in their wake.

Inking: As lone hunters, the giant squids use visual communication for diversion and escape from anything larger than themselves. One of their favorite tactics is expelling ink as a concealing cloud while they dart into the darkness.

Echolocation: Sperm whales use louder and more precise echolocation than in other cetaceans during group hunting and coordinated tracking of challenging prey, like giant squids.

Acoustic Stealth: Giant squid can “freeze” vertically in place when they sense a predator hunting with echolocation in the dark.

By holding a narrow, upright profile in the midwater, the squid makes its outline harder for a scanning sperm whale to resolve.

This is a highly sophisticated precocial behavior (an instinct that animals practice that aren’t taught by their parents).

Social bonds and codas: Every sperm whale pod builds identity through its own pattern of calls, or codas, which help family members recognize and stay bonded to one another.

Learning from the pod: Young whales “babble” with relatives until they learn to speak their ancestors’ language with the timing and rhythm their family expects.

Frequent hunting, guided by elders: As warm-blooded mammals, sperm whales need far more calories than similarly sized predators, so they hunt often and depend on experienced pod elders for feedback during dives.

Lessons Learned

Current research technology is imperfect: Videos are grainy, and sound is not the best quality. This paints an incomplete picture of a deep-sea hunt. We need a combination of more dives and higher-quality recording technology.

We need more data from more hunts spread across world oceans, since the habitat range of sperm whales and giant squids is huge. Only recording hunts from sperm whale cows and pods can create a bias in our understanding of how sperm whales hunt.

More sperm whale bulls need to be recorded somehow to prevent data bias, but they are difficult to study with our current technology and our current camera-mounting techniques, as they can challenge and even ram small research boats.

Moving Forward

Giant squids and sperm whales have survived using specialized tools: sperm whales communicate and hunt with sound, and giant squid hide and navigate with camouflage, excellent vision, speed, inking, and other adaptations to survive full-time in the deep sea. Their hidden world becomes more relatable to us, revealing surprising parallels between their lives and ours: communication and problem-solving as a means for survival. Both altricial animals, like sperm whales (P. macrocephalus), and precocial animals, like giant squids (A. dux), need a stable environment for maintaining stable populations. In the case of sperm whales, a stable population, safe hunting grounds, plentiful food, and space away from human activities helps the passage of cultural knowledge from pod leaders: The whale matriarchs. Areas that are disturbed by sound pollution, heavy sea ship traffic and exploitation of deep-sea fisheries all negatively impact the population sizes and health of predators, such as giant squids and sperm whales. How can we help recover their populations despite continuous survival threats? Gaining insights on the secret lives of these animals provides an opportunity to care about these animals, protect the ecosystems they depend on, and make choices that help ensure their deep-ocean world does not disappear back into silence.

Calls to Action

Modern Conflicts:

  • Sperm Whales: Conflicts between human technology and communication have directly and indirectly killed scores of sperm whales throughout the past several centuries; including fishing gear entanglement, direct hunting, sound pollution, ship strikes, and ingestion of plastic waste.
  • Giant Squids: Giant squid populations appear to remain consistent at the moment, but they are sensitive to acoustic damage that can permanently cause tissue destruction. They also demonstrate sensitivity to low-oxygen environments partially caused by warming ocean temperatures.

How We Can All Help, Today:

  • Vote for policy that restricts development or transportation across open oceans that generates noise pollution for cetaceans and for giant squids.
  • Volunteer for Citizen Science projects, even if you don’t live near the ocean.
