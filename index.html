Introduction and Disclaimer, if needed:

This is a work of fiction, but the creators of this story are a group of scientists and designers dedicated to scientific accuracy. While our main characters, the sperm whales, giant squids, and the scientists studying them, are fictional, our team went through great lengths to ensure that all facts about whales, squid and the way we study them comes from peer-reviewed, or otherwise trusted sources.

Welcome to a very special learning experience, where we follow marine researchers, who follow a sperm whale hunting a giant squid off the coast of current-day Spain. Please join us as we virtually piggyback on one of the largest predators on the planet, using cutting-edge science to reveal their secrets for survival in a changing world.

1. Hero/Myth

  • For centuries, sailors have told legends about whales battling giant monsters beneath the waves. Breaching and beached sperm whales bore the scars of battles in the deep dark, sparking myths of lurking, primordial behemoths that lingered long past the age of dinosaurs. Predating modern marine science, Aristotle described a giant squid, and the Norse told the legend of the Kraken. But sailors could only describe what they could see - strange beaks, scars on whales, giant tentacles rising out of the water- leaving enough mystery for them to create legends about what may lie beneath the waves.
  • Today, we know that there are real animals behind these myths: Meet the sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus ) and giant squid (Archetuethis dux). The real story is much more interesting: Both animals are locked in an evolutionary arms race, constantly adapting to better capture prey and evade other predators. Our subjects are historically misunderstood animals that are nearly opposites of each other, showcasing a spectrum of survival strategies millions of years in the making.
  • The giant squid, Archetuethis dux, is a visually-driven, precocial and solitary invertebrate that is notoriously challenging to study. In today’s exhibit, a giant squid is pursued by a sound-driven, altricial, social predator (the sperm whale, Physeter macrocephalus) in a challenging yet massively biodiverse habitat: The mesopelagic zone, also known as the “Twilight zone.” Both altricial and precocial animals are well-adapted survivors; the “sea monsters” of old are sophisticated examples of altricial versus precocial survival strategies.

2.a. Meet the Scientists

  • Today, we’re following the expedition of cetologists in coastal Spain who want to learn more about sperm whale hunting behavior. Wait, what’s a “cetologist?” Over the past several hundred years, the field of marine biology has branched into a myriad of specific disciplines of study, including cetology (the study of cetaceans; marine mammals like sperm whales) and teuthology, the study of marine cephalopods, which are a diverse group of molluscs like our new friend, the giant squid! These niche fields of study lead to career paths that answer the deepest questions about what really goes in the deep ocean food webs, and how it affects global ocean ecosystems.
  • Cetologists need to understand how sperm whales communicate during a hunt, but the sea does not give up secrets easily. Extreme temperatures and high pressure of the mesopelagic zone- where sperm whales hunt for giant squid- make sperm whales challenging to research. Our cetologist team developed some specialized tools for this expedition. Some tools were very advanced, like an underwater camera that could withstand high pressures with several different types of sensors on it. Other tools were surprisingly simple - like a stick with a suction cup on the end that would firmly attach the deep-sea camera onto a sperm whale.

2. b. Meet the Tech

  • What if we could apply the same types of tools that already help us locate, record and study land-based (terrestrial) wildlife to ocean animals (marine wildlife)? Consider animals that you see every day- it could be birds in your backyard, pond wildlife at your local park, or family pets. Every day, we observe them solving problems, playing, and hunting. Tools such as binoculars, camera collars, magnifying glasses, microphones, drones, and motion-sensing trail cameras can uncover secrets of their hidden lives. Why can’t we use smarter and stronger versions of these tools to discover more about some of the oceans’ largest and most advanced hunters?
  • All of this equipment would help us see and hear sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus ) communicating socially and interacting with prey. But, even with all this equipment in hand, applying it in practice would be challenging: About one-third of a sperm whale's body length is its head, with internal structures akin to a battering ram- which supports old whaler stories of sperm whales sinking ships. Any adult sperm whale could sink the cetologists’ small watercraft in a split second. Despite that, researchers must get close enough to attach the suction-mounted deep sea camera on a sperm whale to further explore the mesopelagic zone and film a giant squid hunt. It’s also important to note that the goal is to not harm these animals, so a small and temporary visual tool like a suction camera is perfect for the job!

3. The Dive

  • Our cetologists access a global knowledge base that identifies a coastal-dwelling pod of sperm whales near Spain. Preparations are made to launch the small watercraft equipped with the suction tools and camera equipment. By the end of the afternoon, a cetologist manages to attach a suction-cup camera to an adult female sperm whale (also called a “cow”) - the matriarch of her pod (a family unit of adult females and their offspring with occasional transient, visitor adult males (“bulls”)). The sperm whale matriarch turns downward and begins to descend into the mesopelagic zone with her pod. The small watercraft retreats to the larger research vessel, and the cetologists join the crew to watch the hunt unfold on the computer.
  • Aboard the gently-rocking research vessel and reunited with the crew, our cetologists receive feedback from the mounted camera: They can hear clicking, a telltale sign of echolocation. The diving sperm whale pod is searching for squid. As the matriarch and her calves click to locate their prey, the giant squid sees a stirred wake of bioluminescence as the pod approaches him in the darkness, and he prepares to use all of his defenses to evade and misdirect. His countershading and inking have provided him with safety and evasion in the past, but this encounter with predators is different. They know his tricks. As he attempts to slow his jet swimming and compose himself vertically, reducing his acoustic cross-section to attempt to “hide” in plain sight, it is too little too late. Five years of fast growth, masterful hunting practices and sophisticated evasion tactics were over for this giant squid in a matter of seconds.
  • As cetologists analyze the data from above, they hear search-clicks tighten into a creak as the sperm whale pod closes in. One of the calves is able to snag the squid - it turns out the matriarch leading the hunt is old, toothless, and teaching her grandchildren how to hunt! The dive was a success for the pod, and the camera tilts. A pale giant squid arm trails from the side of one of the sperm whales’ jaws as more pod members enter the camera view. The surface draws near- concluding a nearly 40-minute expedition. Once the camera and sensor pops off the whale, the cetologists aboard a small watercraft retrieve it. The pod resumes their post-dive socializing at the surface, spouting, rolling and playing under the darkening sky.

4. The Science of Survival

  • The research team begins to understand not just who these animals are, but how they use communication, perception, and specialized tools to survive in darkness and depth. Perhaps this isn’t a monster story, and more of a story about two contrasting species that are alien to us. One species is born with skills to evade capture, and one that passes those lessons down through generations. It turns out that these legendary “sea monsters” of old are both highly sophisticated survivalists in a changing world.
  • All squids- including Archetuethis dux- are precocial, while sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus) are altricial. Let’s examine the evolutionary arms race between these seemingly-opposite masters of the deep:

4. a . Examining Giant Squid Survival Tools:

  • Scanning the darkness with the largest eyes in the animal kingdom, giant squids (Archetuethis dux) focus on bioluminescent trails- the “soup” of bioluminescent plankton that is disturbed and illuminated by a pod of diving sperm whales, like the spinning, rolling wake behind a speedboat or a flurry of snowflakes. A large disturbance suggests a large animal approaching- so the squid must decide to freeze in place or flee with jet propulsion, leaving a cloud of ink in their wake. An approaching sperm whale leaves swirling bioluminescence in its wake, while a fleeing giant squid leaves only darkness.
  • Unable to “hear” a sperm whale's high-frequency echolocation that they use for long-range communication, a suspicious squid can adopt a vertical position in the midwater, reducing its acoustic cross-section as its predators approach and “scan” for it with echolocation. This is a highly sophisticated and precocial behavior (an instinctive survival tool that giant squids practice throughout their lives and aren’t taught by their parents). As asocial lone hunters, the giant squids use visual communication for diversion and escape from anything larger than themselves.

With a typical lifespan of around five years, these huge invertebrates use the following physical and behavioral tools to survive full-time in the mesopelagic zone:

  • Advanced sensory tissue lining the surfaces of their mantle and arms to detect changes in water pressure that betray the movement of a nearby predator or prey
  • Cantaloupe-sized eyes to see further in the darkness than their competitors
  • Speed via jet propulsion
  • Color-changing ability
  • Sonar-evasion technique of freezing vertically in place when encountering huge predators; an example of “behavioral crypsis.”

4. b. Examining Sperm Whale Survival Tools:

Instead of being born with a bag of tricks like giant squids, sperm whales are born with curious minds and a desire to babble their pod’s coda until they speak their ancestors’ language properly. To master the coda is to bond with the pod, and to succeed in the daily hunts. As mammals, sperm whales must consume far more calories in a day than a similarly-sized shark or giant squid to maintain in eternal body temperatures suitable for homeostasis. This means frequent practice dives with lots of guidance and feedback from the pod elders. Where better to seek prey multiple times per day than the diverse and abundant mesopelagic zone? All you need are the right tools to enter this challenging- but rewarding- environment.

Sperm whales also have a handful of adaptations that physically allow them to dive deeper and longer than most other cetaceans, as well as capture and digest some of the world’s largest invertebrates:

  • Concentrated myoglobin in their muscles
  • Partially-collapsible lungs
  • Louder and more precise echolocation than in other cetaceans
  • Digestion of squid beaks and arm hooks using ambergris

5. Lessons Learned

What We Learned:

  1. This technology is young and imperfect: Videos are grainy, and sound is not the best quality, painting an incomplete picture of the hunt. We need a combination of more dives and higher-quality recording technology.
  2. We need more data from broader global sources, since the range of sperm whales is huge. Only recording hunts from cows and pods can skew our understanding of how populations hunt. More bulls need to be recorded somehow to prevent data bias, but they are challenging to camera mount with our current technology and our current techniques.

Moving Forward:

  • Both giant squids and sperm whales have survived using their own extraordinary tools: sperm whales communicating and hunting with sound, and giant squid hiding and navigating with camouflage, vision, and other deep-sea adaptations. Their hidden world became more relatable to us, revealing surprising parallels between their lives and ours: communication, problem-solving, family structures, and the use of tools to find, evade, and endure.
  • Both altricial animals, like sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus), and precocial animals, like giant squids (Archetuethis dux), need a stable environment for maintaining stable populations, and, in the case of sperm whales, for cultural sustenance. Areas that are disturbed by sonar pollution, heavy sea ship traffic and exploitation of deep-sea fisheries all negatively impact population sizes, health and behaviors of top predators, such as giant squids and sperm whales. How can we help recover their populations despite contemporary survival threats? What does the future hold for giant squids, who are affected by humans as well? Gaining insights on the secret lives of these animals provides an opportunity to care about these animals, protect the ecosystems they depend on, and make choices that help ensure their deep-ocean world does not disappear back into silence.

6. Calls to Action

The Conflicts:

  • Conflicts between human technology and communication have directly and indirectly killed scores of sperm whales throughout the past several centuries; including fishing gear entanglement, direct hunting, sound pollution, ship strikes, and ingestion of plastic waste.
  • Giant squid populations appear to remain consistent at the moment, but they are sensitive to acoustic damage that can permanently cause tissue destruction. They also demonstrate sensitivity to low-oxygen environments partially caused by warming ocean temperatures.

How We Can All Help, Today:

  1. Vote for policy that restricts development or transportation across open oceans that generates noise pollution for cetaceans and for giant squids.
  2. Volunteer for Citizen Science projects, even if you don’t live near the ocean.
  3. If you eat seafood, use the Monterey Bay Aquarium’s Seafood Watch pocket guide to make good seafood choices and support sustainable fisheries when shopping or dining out.

Exhibit Wrap Up:

Exhibit Wrap Up:

From all of us at the Museum of Ocean Sciences, we would like to thank you for joining us today on this extraordinary journey to the deep and back!

Entrenched in an evolutionary arms race, sperm whales and giant squids coexisted as predators and prey for millions of years, only to be challenged by the emergence of a new expert communicator: humankind. Recently, humankind has entered a new era of exploration and, at times, exploitation, wielding communication as a tool to supersede basic survival. Through the engagement with a thrilling (but fictional) case study of a sperm whale pod hunting a giant squid in the mesopelagic zone off of the coast of contemporary Spain, we discovered the power of communication and learned behaviors can change the success of a hunt, a mission, and two species’ long-term perseverance.
